A salamander loses a leg and grows a new one.

You lose a fingertip and get a scar.

For 100 years, biologists assumed mammals traded away the power to regenerate somewhere back in evolution. Gone. Permanent.

In 2026, two separate research teams just proved that assumption wrong, and the truth is far stranger:

The ability to regrow lost body parts was never deleted from our biology.

It was just switched OFF.

→ A team in Beijing (with BGI-Research), publishing in Science, found that mice still carry the exact gene salamanders use to regenerate. They'd only lost the "remote control" that fires it after an injury. They reconnected the switch. The mice regrew ear tissue they should never have been able to regrow.

→ Independently, Texas A&M researchers used two well-timed signaling proteins (both already FDA-approved or in human trials) to make mice regrow bone, joints, ligaments and tendons after amputation.

Two teams. Two totally different methods. One jaw-dropping conclusion:

The regeneration program is still inside us. Obscured, not absent.

Here's why this matters far beyond biology 👇

The biggest breakthroughs often aren't about inventing something new.

They're about realizing the capability was there all along, quietly disabled, and asking a better question:

Not "how do we build this?"
But "why was this turned off… and what happens if we turn it back on?"

That mindset applies to a lot more than medicine.

What have you been trying to build, when the real move was to un-silence something that already exists?
