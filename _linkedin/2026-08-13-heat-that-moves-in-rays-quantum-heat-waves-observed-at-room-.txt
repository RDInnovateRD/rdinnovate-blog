For 70 years, chip design has run on one assumption: heat spreads.

UCLA engineers just broke it.

They guided heat through a crystal in tight, focused rays. Like a beam of light. At room temperature.

Not near absolute zero in a cryogenic lab. At the same 300 kelvin your laptop runs at.

The material is boron arsenide. Weak phonon scattering, so the vibrations that carry heat stop behaving like a diffusing gas and start behaving like a wave you can aim. Sixfold, eightfold, and fourfold focusing patterns, set by the crystal itself.

Why it matters:

Modern AI chips don't fail because they run out of compute. They fail because a few dense hot spots run out of ways to shed heat. If you can route that heat along a planned path instead of letting it smear everywhere, you relax one of the tightest ceilings in hardware.

The bigger lesson has nothing to do with heat.

Most stubborn engineering limits are material limits wearing a disguise. The whole industry optimized around "heat spreads" as if it were a law of nature. Change the material and it turns into a design choice.

The best R&D teams don't ask how to beat the constraint faster. They ask whether the constraint was ever real.

What "law of nature" in your field is actually just an untested material assumption?
