## The challenge of ocean carbon accounting

Proposals to leverage marine chemistry for large-scale carbon dioxide removal frequently assume that geochemical interventions act as simple, single-parameter modifications to seawater chemistry. Geochemical strategies targeting billion-tonne-scale carbon removal generally rely on altering the equilibrium of the ocean’s carbonate system. In seawater, dissolved inorganic carbon exists in a dynamic equilibrium comprising dissolved gas, carbonic acid, bicarbonate ions, and carbonate ions. The balance between these chemical species is governed primarily by total alkalinity—a measure of the ocean's capacity to neutralise acid—and the concentration of total dissolved inorganic carbon.

Existing research and operational concepts generally divide ocean-based geochemical removal into distinct approaches. Ocean alkalinity enhancement seeks to increase seawater alkalinity by adding basic minerals or chemical compounds. This shifts the chemical equilibrium to convert dissolved carbon dioxide into stable bicarbonate and carbonate ions, lowering the partial pressure of carbon dioxide in surface waters and inducing the ocean to absorb additional carbon dioxide from the atmosphere. Conversely, direct ocean removal targets the carbon parameter directly, extracting dissolved inorganic carbon from seawater using chemical, physical, or electrochemical processes. This reduction in total dissolved carbon similarly lowers surface water carbon dioxide pressure, driving compensatory atmospheric absorption.

However, evaluating these approaches as isolated chemical interventions creates a mismatch with real-world implementation. Many promising land-based and coastal interventions—such as enhanced rock weathering, riverine alkalinity enhancement, and the accelerated weathering of limestone—do not deliver pure alkalinity directly into the open ocean. Instead, alkaline materials are introduced into soils, rivers, or coastal margins. During transport through terrestrial and freshwater environments, these added alkaline compounds partially equilibrate with ambient atmospheric carbon dioxide before entering marine waters. By the time these solutions discharge into the sea, they deliver a simultaneous influx of both total alkalinity and dissolved inorganic carbon. Treating these inputs as simple alkalinity additions overstates their net carbon removal capacity, highlighting the need for assessment frameworks capable of handling coupled geochemical modifications.

## Integrating alkalinity and carbon dynamics

To address these evaluation challenges, a unified assessment framework has been established using a global ocean biogeochemical model. The framework systematically maps and compares the carbon removal efficiencies of ocean alkalinity enhancement, direct ocean removal, and their combined manifestation, termed combined alkalinity and dissolved inorganic carbon addition.

The core analytical capability of this framework stems from demonstrating that individual simulations of ocean alkalinity enhancement and direct ocean removal can be mathematically combined to predict the net behaviour of combined alkalinity and carbon additions. When alkalinity is added alone, it enhances the oceanic storage capacity for carbon without immediately altering the total amount of dissolved carbon present. When dissolved inorganic carbon is removed alone, it creates a carbon deficit relative to existing alkalinity. When both parameters are modified simultaneously—as occurs during the partial equilibration of weathered rock products—the net efficiency depends on the relative balance and interaction of both changes within marine waters.

By running baseline simulations for individual alkalinity additions and carbon removals, the framework allows researchers to superimpose these datasets to quantify the oceanic fate of complex, combined perturbations. This approach makes it possible to model pathways like riverine alkalinity enhancement and limestone weathering without needing to build customized, isolated models for every specific land-to-ocean delivery mechanism.

Furthermore, the global model maps how these efficiencies vary across geography and seasonal cycles. Ocean surface waters undergo significant temporal and spatial variations in temperature, salinity, mixing layer depth, and baseline carbon saturation. The framework demonstrates that the timing and location of geochemical perturbations heavily influence carbon uptake rates. Ocean regions characterised by deep vertical mixing or specific seasonal temperature changes respond differently to chemical modifications than warm, stable surface waters. Mapping these spatial and seasonal dependencies provides a standardised benchmark for comparing where and when specific geochemical interventions will yield the highest carbon removal efficiency.

## Model boundaries and deployment realities

While this unified framework represents a step forward for standardised ocean carbon accounting, its findings must be interpreted within the context of numerical modelling boundaries. The framework quantifies theoretical geochemical efficiency based on global ocean transport and baseline biogeochemical dynamics, but it does not represent direct empirical validation in physical marine environments.

Global ocean biogeochemical models operate at broad spatial and temporal resolutions. Consequently, they aggregate fine-scale coastal processes, near-shore mixing dynamics, and complex sediment-water interactions. In localized coastal environments where riverine inputs or rock weathering products discharge, high concentrations of alkalinity and dissolved inorganic carbon can trigger secondary chemical reactions, such as the precipitation of calcium carbonate. Secondary precipitation consumes alkalinity and releases carbon dioxide, effectively neutralizing a portion of the intended carbon removal. Global circulation models cannot fully capture these micro-scale boundary layer dynamics, meaning local deployment efficiencies may deviate from global model projections.

Additionally, the model evaluates chemical efficiency—the amount of atmospheric carbon absorbed per unit of geochemical alteration—rather than complete lifecycle feasibility. It assumes specific perturbations occur without modelling the upstream energy inputs, material mining requirements, grinding processes, or transportation infrastructure necessary to achieve billion-tonne deployment scales. Furthermore, verifying these modelled outputs in open-ocean environments presents formidable challenges for measurement, reporting, and verification systems. Environmental factors such as ocean turbulence, biological carbon pumps, and rapid atmospheric exchange make tracking small geochemical signals against background marine noise extremely difficult in real-world conditions.

## The R&D takeaway

R&D leaders funding or planning marine carbon dioxide removal technologies should transition away from single-variable evaluation metrics and adopt integrated modelling frameworks that account for coupled chemical changes. Early-stage project designs for weathering and riverine interventions must explicitly model the partial equilibration of alkalinity with carbon dioxide prior to oceanic entry. Project roadmaps should leverage global spatial and seasonal efficiency mapping to select optimal release locations and seasons, while pairing numerical model projections with high-resolution coastal monitoring to validate local geochemical dynamics.
