## The static lattice assumption in fast ionic conductors

Conventional models of solid-state battery electrolytes treat ionic transport as a two-part system: mobile positive cations hopping through an unyielding, stationary crystalline framework. In this traditional view, the host lattice provides fixed geometric pathways and static energetic barriers, while diffusing ions navigate the immobile structure. However, solid-state materials engineered for high ionic conductivity frequently possess substantial structural disorder, and how this mesoscale disorder actively influences ion motion has remained an open question.

The core difficulty stems from traditional characterisation methods. Standard bulk measurement tools aggregate structural signals across macroscopic sample volumes and extended timescales. By smoothing out localized, time-dependent fluctuations, these ensemble measurements reinforce the assumption that the host crystal lattice remains rigid while cations migrate. Whether the surrounding framework undergoes rapid structural reorganisations on the same temporal scale as ion diffusion has consequently been difficult to observe directly.

A study reported in an arXiv preprint—which has not yet been peer reviewed—addresses this diagnostic limitation by probing the nanoscale structural dynamics of sodium thiophosphate, a fast-conducting solid electrolyte. By isolating an individual microparticle rather than analyzing a bulk ensemble, the researchers observed structural fluctuations operating directly at the nanoscale, demonstrating that the host lattice is far more dynamic than static transport models assume.

## Nanoscale dynamics in sodium thiophosphate

To track structural changes within an individual material grain, the researchers used Bragg X-ray photon correlation spectroscopy. This technique allowed them to measure the internal nanoscale dynamics of a single ball-milled particle of cubic sodium thiophosphate. Mechanical ball milling is standard in material synthesis to introduce structural disorder and modify grain boundaries, both of which strongly affect ionic transport properties.

The spectroscopic measurements revealed that the cubic phase of sodium thiophosphate behaves as a strain glass. Rather than maintaining a uniform, long-range cubic symmetry, the material forms a frustrated structural configuration consisting of three degenerate tetragonal variants that exhibit short-range order. This geometric frustration creates a network of internal domain boundaries throughout the crystal particle.

The motion of these domain walls is initially impelled by residual mechanical stresses retained within the particle from the ball-milling process. When the material is subjected to thermal annealing, these residual milling stresses are progressively depleted. As internal stress relaxes, the domain-wall motion undergoes an irreversible crossover, shifting from stress-driven dynamics to fluctuations propelled purely by thermal energy.

Furthermore, the structural reorganisations within the strain glass proceed through scale-free avalanche statistics. This collective, intermittent relaxation behaviour mirrors the structural dynamics seen in ferroelastic martensitic alloys. However, while classic ferroelastic martensites require external mechanical driving forces to induce domain avalanches, the strain-glass phase of sodium thiophosphate undergoes these collective reorganisations spontaneously without any external mechanical load.

## Matching host relaxation to cation diffusion

The presence of strain-glass dynamics in the cubic phase becomes even more distinct when compared directly against other structural forms of the same chemical compound. The ordered tetragonal polymorph of sodium thiophosphate displays none of these strain-glass characteristics or scale-free avalanches. Lacking the frustrated domain network of the cubic phase, the host lattice of the tetragonal polymorph relaxes roughly fifty times more slowly.

The central physical finding of the study lies in comparing host lattice relaxation rates directly against cation movement. By matching the temporal relaxation rates of the cubic lattice with the estimated time required for a sodium ion to diffuse across the microparticle, the authors found that both processes occur on comparable timescales.

This temporal overlap challenges a foundational premise of solid-state electrolyte modeling. When host lattice domain walls move, transform, and reorganise at speeds comparable to the transit time of diffusing cations, the structural framework cannot be modeled as a passive, static background. Instead, local lattice distortions and collective structural avalanches actively alter the atomic environment and energetic pathways experienced by mobile ions during transport.

## Real-world limitations and translation barriers

While these observations provide a clearer microscopic picture of solid-state ionic conduction, several fundamental and engineering caveats remain relevant for energy technology development. First and foremost, the research is detailed in a preprint that has not yet undergone peer review, meaning its experimental interpretations and dynamic models require independent verification by the broader scientific community.

Second, the experimental scope is strictly focused on fundamental nanoscale structural physics within a single ball-milled microparticle. The study demonstrates localized lattice motion under precise X-ray characterisation conditions, but it does not evaluate macro-scale battery performance, electrochemical stability windows, interfacial resistance against metal anodes, or long-term battery cycling durability.

Third, translating observations of strain-glass dynamics into practical electrolyte manufacturing presents non-trivial material control challenges. Ball milling introduces heterogeneous internal stress fields that vary between individual particles, and controlling these frustrated domain networks during scaled industrial synthesis will require carefully optimized processing windows. Incorporating host-lattice dynamics into practical battery design will also require developing new transport models that explicitly couple mobile cation trajectories with dynamic, fluctuating crystal frames.

## The R&D takeaway

For managers directing energy storage material portfolios, these findings indicate that computational screeners relying on static host-lattice approximations may incorrectly model the physical mechanics of fast ion conduction. R&D strategy should account for mesoscale host-lattice mobility when evaluating disordered solid-state electrolytes. Investing in characterisation protocols that resolve time-dependent lattice fluctuations will be critical for guiding the rational synthesis of next-generation solid electrolytes.
