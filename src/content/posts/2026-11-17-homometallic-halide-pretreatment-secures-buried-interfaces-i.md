Inverted perovskite solar cells have reached laboratory power conversion efficiencies that rival those of established silicon photovoltaics, but their operational durability under realistic outdoor conditions remains a significant technical barrier. In an inverted (p-i-n) architecture, light passes through a transparent conductive oxide layer at the bottom of the device before reaching the light-absorbing perovskite layer and upper electrical contacts. To extract charge carriers efficiently across this junction, thin interfacial films known as self-assembled monolayers are deposited onto the conductive oxide surface. While these organic monolayers provide key energy-level alignment and electronic passivation, the interface between the inorganic oxide substrate and the organic monolayer represents a structural vulnerability. Outdoor solar panels experience broad diurnal and seasonal temperature swings, causing adjacent materials with differing thermal expansion properties to expand and contract at unequal rates.

Recent work reported in a preprint—which has not yet been peer-reviewed—identifies stress accumulation at this buried self-assembled monolayer interface as a primary failure mode in inverted perovskite devices. When exposed to repeated temperature fluctuations, thermo-mechanical stresses concentrate at the junction where the self-assembled monolayer anchors to the underlying transparent conductive oxide. Over time, these localized mechanical forces overcome the bond strength at the interface, causing microscopic delamination, loss of mechanical adhesion, and a rapid decline in power conversion efficiency. Addressing this mechanical instability requires engineering the interfacial chemistry so that stress can be distributed effectively across the material boundary without disrupting charge extraction.

## Homometallic halide pretreatment and hierarchical anchoring

To prevent stress-induced failure at the buried interface, the authors developed a chemical modification technique based on a homometallic halide pretreatment applied directly to the transparent conductive oxide surface prior to monolayer deposition. The primary function of this pretreatment is to transform the surface chemistry of the conductive oxide substrate into a highly receptive template that guides the ordered assembly of the self-assembled monolayer.

The treatment systematically increases the density of surface hydroxyl species across the transparent conductive oxide. These newly populated hydroxyl sites provide reactive positions that alter how subsequent monolayer molecules attach to the substrate. Instead of forming sparse or irregular chemical links, the self-assembled monolayer establishes a dense, hierarchical, multipoint anchoring network.

This anchoring mechanism operates through two cooperative chemical pathways. The dominant binding force consists of metal-oxygen coordination bonds formed directly between the substrate and the functional groups of the monolayer. Working alongside these coordination bonds are hydrogen-bonding interactions induced by chloride ions introduced during the halide pretreatment. The combination of metal-oxygen coordination and chloride-mediated hydrogen bonding creates a multi-anchored structural matrix at the interface.

From a mechanical perspective, this multipoint architecture converts what was previously a series of isolated anchor points into a continuous load-sharing network. When temperature shifts induce physical strain between the rigid oxide substrate and the adjacent organic layers, the applied forces are distributed broadly across multiple coordinated binding sites. By avoiding localized stress concentration, the modified interface exhibits enhanced tolerance to thermo-mechanical fatigue.

## Thermal cycling performance and current limits

The application of this hierarchical anchoring strategy delivered high efficiency and improved endurance in laboratory test devices. Unmodified devices typically suffer rapid efficiency degradation when subjected to rapid temperature changes due to interfacial shear failure. In contrast, solar cells incorporating the homometallic halide pretreatment achieved an initial power conversion efficiency of 27.3 per cent, with an independently verified certified efficiency of 26.9 per cent.

To evaluate long-term durability under thermal stress, the researchers subjected the pretreated cells to accelerated environmental testing following the ISOS-T-3 standard protocol. This protocol subjects photovoltaic devices to repeated temperature cycles ranging between extreme cold (-40 degrees Celsius) and elevated heat (85 degrees Celsius). After 400 continuous thermal cycles, the pretreated inverted perovskite cells maintained 94 per cent of their initial power conversion efficiency, demonstrating that the reinforced buried interface resists stress accumulation over extended thermal cycling.

While these results highlight the value of targeted interface engineering, several operational and manufacturing boundaries remain before such techniques can be applied in commercial module production. First, the reported performance metrics were evaluated on small-area laboratory test cells. Scaling chemical pretreatments to large-area glass substrates demands precise control over solution wetting, chemical reaction kinetics, and surface hydroxyl uniformities across square metres of material, where local variations in coating quality could induce localized failure points.

Second, thermal stress is only one of several degrading factors that solar panels face during outdoor deployment. Although 400 ISOS-T-3 cycles demonstrate resistance to thermo-mechanical stress, commercial module standards require durability over thousands of thermal cycles alongside prolonged exposure to ultraviolet light, moisture ingress, and continuous electrical bias. The study focuses specifically on stress accumulation at the bottom conductive oxide interface, meaning that degradation mechanisms occurring within the perovskite absorber layer itself or at the top contact interface must be addressed separately to achieve multi-decade operational lifetimes.

## The R&D takeaway

Interface engineering in thin-film photovoltaics must balance electronic passivation with mechanical stress resilience. Research teams developing organic-inorganic heterojunctions should incorporate thermo-mechanical stress testing early into material screening pipelines, rather than treating interface delamination purely as a packaging issue. By designing chemical pretreatments that establish multipoint, cooperative bonding networks, R&D programs can build structural tolerance directly into buried interfaces without sacrificing charge-carrier extraction efficiency.
