Standard clinical trial methodology relies on the assumption that a research protocol acts as a neutral diagnostic tool. In this classical framework, trial investigators recruit study participants, administer protocol-defined interventions, and track outcomes under structured conditions, leaving baseline clinical practice outside the trial unchanged until results are formally analysed and published. The clinical protocol is treated as an isolated measurement instrument designed to test an intervention without altering the daily habits or decision-making thresholds of the practitioners running the study.

However, complex surgical trials rarely operate in an isolated vacuum. Operating surgeons who participate in pragmatic clinical trials gain direct, hands-on experience with procedures or management strategies they might otherwise perform infrequently or avoid altogether. This operational involvement creates an active learning process while data collection is still underway. If trial enrolment alone shifts clinician confidence, alters risk perception, or broadens candidate eligibility criteria before any outcome data is published, the assumed separation between trial execution and real-world clinical behavior breaks down. Understanding this phenomenon is essential for interpreting trial dynamics, evaluating clinical equipoise, and designing pragmatic research programs.

## Measuring trial participation as a driver of clinical change

To examine how trial enrolment directly alters surgical decision-making prior to the publication of study findings, a recent investigation surveyed clinical investigators who participated in the INFection ORthopaedic Management (INFORM) trial. The INFORM trial was designed as a pragmatic randomised controlled trial to evaluate single-stage revision surgery against two-stage revision surgery in patients with hip periprosthetic joint infection.

Treating a periprosthetic joint infection surrounding an orthopaedic hip implant is a complex surgical challenge. The traditional approach, known as two-stage revision, involves removing the infected hardware, inserting a temporary cement spacer, administering systemic antibiotics, and performing a second procedure months later to implant a definitive prosthesis. Single-stage revision combines the removal of the infected implant and the insertion of a new permanent prosthesis into a single operative session. While single-stage revision reduces the overall surgical burden on the patient, it requires specific technical comfort and precise patient selection.

To determine whether recruiting patients into the trial influenced clinician perspectives, an electronic survey was administered to all surgeons who participated in and recruited patients for the INFORM trial. The survey was issued after trial recruitment had officially closed, capturing practitioner perspectives at a point when formal trial outcomes were still unknown. The survey evaluated self-reported changes in overall practice, confidence in executing single-stage procedures, and specific clinical indications for recommending single-stage revision. Quantitative responses were summarised descriptively, while open-ended text fields were evaluated using descriptive content analysis to identify key underlying themes.

## Quantitative shifts in surgeon confidence and intervention criteria

A total of 26 trial-participating surgeons completed the survey. Analysis of the quantitative items revealed notable shifts in clinical attitudes across multiple dimensions, demonstrating that participation in the trial served as an active driver of practice adaptation long before final efficacy or safety data were released.

When asked about their general approach to managing hip periprosthetic joint infections, 11 of the 26 respondents (42.3 per cent) reported that their overall opinion comparing single-stage to two-stage revision had changed as a direct result of participating in the trial. When survey questions focused on technical confidence and intervention criteria, the reported practice shifts were even more prominent. Sixteen surgeons (61.5 per cent) reported an increase in their confidence to perform single-stage revision procedures.

In addition, 18 of the 26 surgeons (69.2 per cent) indicated a greater willingness to offer single-stage revision to patients for whom they previously would not have considered the procedure prior to participating in the trial. This expansion of clinical eligibility extended to high-risk and complex clinical scenarios. Following trial participation, surgeons reported increased willingness to consider single-stage revision in the presence of local soft-tissue complications, such as an active sinus tract or an abscess, as well as in cases involving challenging bacterial pathogens such as Enterococcus infection. Participating in the trial protocol effectively broadened the clinical scope for single-stage revision across presentations that surgeons previously managed with traditional two-stage strategies.

## Qualitative mechanisms and clinical trial design limits

To clarify the mechanisms driving these quantitative shifts, descriptive content analysis was applied to the surgeons' open-ended survey entries. The analysis identified four inter-related qualitative themes that explain how protocol participation reshapes surgical decision-making.

First, surgeons reported increased familiarity and comfort with performing single-stage revision, noting that trial involvement reduced procedural uncertainty. Second, trial participation provided clarification or reinforcement of patient selection criteria, giving clinicians a more defined framework to evaluate candidate suitability. Third, for a subset of respondents, trial participation functioned primarily as a confirmation of their existing surgical practice, reinforcing established clinical routines. Fourth, surgeons emphasized the influence of their local multidisciplinary and institutional context, where collaborative institutional experience and peer discussions within trial centres supported shifts in individual clinical decision-making.

While these findings reveal an important dynamic in surgical research, several limitations must be considered. The study evaluated a sample of 26 surgeons from a single pragmatic trial focused specifically on hip periprosthetic joint infection. Consequently, the sample size is modest and reflects the perspectives of active trial recruiters rather than a broad cross-section of the wider surgical community. Additionally, the data rely on self-reported survey responses regarding clinical confidence and practice shifts, rather than objective audits of operative registries or longitudinal surgical logs. Finally, the survey assessed surgeon attitudes immediately following the conclusion of trial recruitment, meaning it does not determine whether these clinical practice changes persist over extended periods or how surgeons might further adjust their practice once the definitive results of the INFORM trial are published.

## The R&D takeaway

For research directors and clinical program managers, these findings highlight that pragmatic clinical trials function as active interventions in their own right, modifying practitioner behavior and clinical equipoise well before study results are published. R&D planners must account for this protocol-induced experiential learning when modeling recruitment dynamics, baseline standard-of-care controls, and post-trial technology adoption timelines across investigative sites. Incorporating trial participation itself into strategic evaluations of clinical workflows can help refine trial designs, manage operator learning curves, and improve the translation of complex medical procedures into clinical practice.
