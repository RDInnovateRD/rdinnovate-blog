Water treatment strategies for per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) have historically relied on a single design assumption: that capturing long-chain fluorinated molecules using hydrophobic interactions would be sufficient to remediate contaminated water sources. As industrial manufacturing has shifted away from legacy long-chain compounds, environmental waters increasingly contain complex mixtures where short-chain and ultra-short-chain alternatives coexist alongside residual long-chain molecules. Traditional remediation materials, including standard activated carbon and ion exchange resins, struggle in these mixed regimes. Because their surface chemistries are relatively uniform, their binding affinity depends heavily on molecule size. Long-chain PFAS outcompete shorter molecules for available sites, allowing short-chain and ultra-short-chain compounds to pass through treatment systems with minimal retention.

Overcoming this selectivity gap requires moving away from generic hydrophobic adsorbents toward materials whose local surface polarity can be explicitly tuned to match the molecular geometry and hydrophobic characteristics of specific targeted compounds.

## Tailoring molecular interfaces for variable PFAS chains

Perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs) possess a hydrophilic carboxylic acid group attached to a fluorinated carbon tail. As the carbon chain length of the fluorinated tail varies, the overall hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance of the molecule changes dramatically. Ultra-short-chain PFCAs, such as trifluoroacetic acid, exhibit strong hydrophilic characteristics and high solubility in water, making them virtually invisible to standard hydrophobic adsorbents. Conversely, long-chain variants like perfluorononanoic acid feature extensive hydrophobic tails that drive strong surface interactions but easily displace smaller molecules during competitive binding.

To address this structural spectrum, functionalised porous materials offer a platform for precise interfacial engineering. Polyurethane sponges provide a high-surface-area, flexible structural backbone that can be chemically modified. By grafting alkylamine molecules onto the polyurethane substrate, the local hydrophilic-hydrophobic environment around the functional binding sites can be deliberately altered.

The chemical tuning relies on altering the carbon-chain length of the grafted alkylamines. Introducing short alkyl chains yields an interface dominated by polar, hydrophilic interactions, whereas grafting longer alkyl chains creates a localized hydrophobic environment. When these modified polyurethane substrates come into contact with aqueous PFAS, the matching local polarity between the adsorbent surface and the target PFCA molecule governs adsorption efficiency. This structural alignment allows the material to overcome the competitive displacement that typically limits conventional media.

## Matching alkylamine chain lengths to target compounds

Experimental evaluations of three modified polyurethane sponge variants—designated PPU-C2, PPU-C6, and PPU-C14 based on the carbon-chain length of the attached alkylamines—demonstrate how targeted interface regulation dictates removal performance across different PFCA chain lengths.

For ultra-short-chain targets, the short-chain functionalised material PPU-C2 creates a highly polar interface that successfully attracts hydrophilic fluorinated species. When tested against trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), an ultra-short-chain PFCA that typically escapes standard filtration, PPU-C2 achieved a removal efficiency of 72 percent. The maximum calculated adsorption capacity for TFA reached 596 milligrams per gram of adsorbent.

Intermediate chain lengths required a balanced hydrophobic-hydrophobic interface. The mid-length modification, PPU-C6, exhibited its highest affinity toward perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA), a short-chain PFCA. PPU-C6 achieved an 89 percent removal efficiency for PFHxA, with a maximum adsorption capacity of 829.2 milligrams per gram. The matching between the six-carbon alkylamine modification and the six-carbon fluorinated target highlights the importance of structural symmetry in optimising interfacial binding energy.

For long-chain fluorinated compounds, the long-chain modified variant PPU-C14 provided the necessary hydrophobic environment to maximize surface interactions. When exposed to perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), PPU-C14 demonstrated a removal efficiency of 96 percent and a maximum adsorption capacity of 848.2 milligrams per gram.

Across all tested variants, theoretical and experimental characterisations confirmed that the dominant mechanism driving adsorption is a chain-length-dependent matching relationship between the PFCA molecular structure and the local interfacial polarity. Furthermore, testing in natural water samples confirmed that these tailored materials retain their selective adsorption performance despite the presence of background organic matter and dissolved ions. The modified sponges also demonstrated effective reusability over multiple operational cycles.

## Performance boundaries and scaled deployment challenges

While tuning interfacial polarity provides a clear physical pathway for targeted PFCA capture, several operational and material limitations must be resolved before this technology can transition from controlled laboratory testing to industrial wastewater or municipal remediation systems.

First, the experimental evaluations focused on individual representative target compounds—specifically TFA for ultra-short chains, PFHxA for short chains, and PFNA for long chains. Industrial effluents and contaminated groundwater sources rarely present isolated compounds; instead, they contain heterogeneous mixtures containing dozens of fluorinated precursors, perfluoroalkane sulfonates, and non-fluorinated organic contaminants. How these engineered sponge variants perform in multi-component competitive environments where short-chain and long-chain PFCAs coexist simultaneously remains an open question that requires further investigation.

Second, the structural and economic feasibility of deploying functionalised polyurethane sponges at scale presents engineering hurdles. While polyurethane provides an adaptable porous framework, its long-term mechanical stability, resistance to chemical degradation, and hydraulic pressure characteristics under high-volume flow conditions differ significantly from packed beds of granular activated carbon or ion exchange resins. Integrating these sponges into existing water treatment infrastructure may require modular contactor designs rather than standard packed columns.

Third, while the materials demonstrated good reusability in laboratory tests, the desorptive processes required to regenerate the saturated sponges generate highly concentrated fluorinated waste streams. The long-term economic viability of the system will depend heavily on coupling these selective adsorption sponges with effective destruction technologies capable of mineralising the concentrated PFAS back-end effluent.

## The R&D takeaway

R&D leaders evaluating water remediation technologies should shift focus from generic, broad-spectrum adsorbents toward materials designed with tunable interfacial polarity. Engineering adsorbents to target specific molecular chain lengths provides a viable path to address short-chain PFAS breakthrough without sacrificing long-chain capture capacity. Future development programs should prioritize testing functionalised materials in complex, multi-contaminant fluid streams while establishing viable regeneration and concentrated waste destruction workflows.
